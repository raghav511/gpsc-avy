Definitions carry most marks here: the types of wound healing, sinus versus fistula, the kinds of shock and ulcer, and burn depth with the rule of nines.

Shock

Shock is inadequate tissue perfusion. The commonest type after injury is hypovolaemic.

TypeCauseSigns
HypovolaemicBlood or fluid loss (haemorrhage, burns, vomiting, diarrhoea)Cold clammy skin, rapid thready pulse, low BP, low urine output
CardiogenicPump failure (myocardial infarction)Cold skin, raised JVP
Septic (distributive)Infection releasing toxinsEarly warm flushed skin and bounding pulse, later cold
Anaphylactic (distributive)AllergyUrticaria, stridor, wheeze, low BP
Neurogenic (distributive)Spinal cord injuryLow BP with slow pulse, warm skin

Blood loss and signs (ATLS classes): up to 15% (750 mL) little change; 15–30% pulse over 100; 30–40% BP falls; over 40% life-threatening. Urine output under 0.5 mL/kg/h signals poor perfusion. Hook for shock first aid: lie flat, legs raised, keep warm, nothing by mouth, shift to hospital.

Rule of nines (adult): head and neck 9%, each upper limb 9%, front of trunk 18%, back of trunk 18%, each lower limb 18%, perineum 1%. The patient's own palm (with fingers) is about 1% of body surface. In children the head is larger (about 18%) and the legs smaller (Lund and Browder chart). Admit adults with over 15% burns (children over 10%), and any burn of the face, hands, feet, perineum or airway.

First aid for burns: stop the burning, cool with running tap water for 20 minutes (no ice, butter or oil), remove rings and tight clothing, cover with cling film or a clean cloth, keep the patient warm. Face and neck burns are dangerous because of airway swelling.

Add the regions that are burnt: a burn of the whole front of the trunk and one arm is 18 + 9 = 27%.

Perioperative care

  • Before surgery: informed written consent, fasting (solids 6 hours, clear fluids 2 hours), assessment of fitness (ASA grade I–VI), blood sugar and blood pressure control, marking the site.

  • After surgery: watch airway, breathing, circulation, pain, urine output; early mobilisation prevents deep-vein thrombosis and chest infection.

  • Post-operative fever: day 1–2 lung collapse (atelectasis); day 3–5 urinary tract infection or wound infection; day 5–7 deep-vein thrombosis; remember the "5 Ws": wind, water, wound, walking, wonder drugs.

Expect eponymous fractures, the difference between a sprain, strain and dislocation, and the X-ray signs of osteoarthritis and spondylosis.

Fractures

A fracture is a break in the continuity of bone. Signs: pain, swelling, deformity, abnormal mobility, crepitus, loss of function. Closed (skin intact) or open/compound (communicates with the outside, risk of infection).

Healing stages: haematoma → inflammation → soft callus → hard callus → remodelling. Upper limb bones unite in about 6–8 weeks, lower limb in 12 weeks; children heal in half the time. Complications: shock, fat embolism (long bones, 24–72 h), compartment syndrome (pain on passive stretch: the earliest sign), avascular necrosis (scaphoid, femoral head), malunion, non-union, Volkmann's ischaemic contracture (supracondylar fracture of humerus in children).

First aid: immobilise the joint above and below with a splint, do not try to straighten deformity, cover open wounds, treat for shock.

Dislocations and soft-tissue injury

TermMeaning
DislocationComplete loss of contact between joint surfaces
SubluxationPartial loss of contact
SprainInjury to a ligament (commonest: lateral ligament of the ankle, inversion injury)
StrainInjury to a muscle or tendon

Shoulder is the commonest joint to dislocate, anterior in about 95% (axillary nerve at risk). Hip dislocation is usually posterior (dashboard injury; limb flexed, adducted, internally rotated; sciatic nerve at risk). RICE for sprains: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation.

Osteomyelitis

Infection of bone. Acute haematogenous osteomyelitis is commonest in children, in the metaphysis of long bones (lower femur, upper tibia), caused mostly by Staphylococcus aureus. Signs: high fever, severe local pain, refusal to move the limb. X-ray is normal for the first 10–14 days; MRI shows it early. Chronic osteomyelitis: sequestrum (dead bone), involucrum (new bone around it), discharging sinus. Tuberculosis of the spine is Pott's disease (gibbus, cold abscess, paraplegia).

Cervical spondylosis: neck pain and stiffness radiating to the shoulders and arms, numbness and tingling (radiculopathy, commonest at C5–C6); vertigo on turning the head. Lumbar spondylosis / disc prolapse: low back pain with sciatica (L4–L5 and L5–S1), positive straight-leg-raising test. Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis): painful restriction of all shoulder movements, commoner in diabetics.

First aid

Basic life support (adult) — C-A-B. Check response and breathing → call for help → chest compressions 30 : 2 breaths, rate 100–120 per minute, depth 5–6 cm, centre of the chest; continue until help arrives. Recovery position for an unconscious patient who is breathing.
