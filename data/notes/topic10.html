Health and disease

  • WHO definition of health (1948): "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely an absence of disease or infirmity". Spiritual health is a proposed fourth dimension.

  • Epidemiological triad: agent, host and environment. For non-communicable diseases a web of causation (multiple factors) is used instead.

  • Iceberg phenomenon: the visible tip is clinical cases; the submerged part is sub-clinical, undiagnosed and carrier cases. Hypertension, diabetes and malnutrition show a large hidden part.

  • Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health states in populations and its application to their control (Last).

Levels of prevention

Prevention is matched to the stage of disease. Leavell and Clark gave five modes of intervention: health promotion, specific protection, early diagnosis and treatment, disability limitation, and rehabilitation.

Read the chart left to right: primordial prevention acts before any risk factor exists, and tertiary prevention acts once disease has caused disability.

Measuring disease

  • Incidence: new cases in a period ÷ population at risk. It measures risk and is used for acute diseases.

  • Prevalence: all existing cases (old + new) at a time ÷ total population. It is useful for chronic diseases and planning services.

  • Prevalence = Incidence × Mean duration (P = I × D).

  • Attack rate: an incidence rate used during an epidemic. Secondary attack rate = cases among contacts ÷ susceptible contacts; it measures infectiousness.

  • Incubation period: time from infection to first symptom. Serial interval: gap between onset of primary and secondary cases.

  • Herd immunity: a high proportion of immune people protects the unimmunised; it underlies mass immunisation.

Epidemics

  • Common-source, single exposure: cases rise and fall within one incubation period (e.g. food poisoning).

  • Propagated (person to person): gradual rise, several waves (e.g. hepatitis A, measles).

  • Endemic: constant presence in an area. Pandemic: spread over many countries.

  • Sporadic: scattered, irregular cases.

Screening

Use the 2×2 table: TP, FP, FN, TN.

  • Sensitivity = TP ÷ (TP + FN): ability to detect true cases (true positive rate).

  • Specificity = TN ÷ (TN + FP): ability to identify true non-cases.

  • Positive predictive value = TP ÷ (TP + FP). It rises with prevalence.

  • Negative predictive value = TN ÷ (TN + FN).

  • A screening test should be sensitive; a confirmatory (diagnostic) test should be specific.

  • Wilson and Jungner (WHO, 1968) gave the criteria for a screening programme.

Immunisation and the cold chain

  • Active immunity: from infection or vaccine; slow but long-lasting. Passive: ready-made antibodies (e.g. immunoglobulin, maternal antibodies); immediate but short.

  • Cold chain: vaccines are kept at +2 to +8 °C in an ice-lined refrigerator (ILR) at the health facility.

  • Most heat-sensitive vaccine: OPV. Freeze-sensitive vaccines (never freeze): Hepatitis B, pentavalent, DPT, Td, IPV.

  • Shake test detects a frozen vaccine. Vaccine vial monitor (VVM): discard when the inner square matches or is darker than the outer circle.

  • The full Universal Immunisation Programme schedule is in the Topic 8 doc.

Genus epidemicus (homoeopathic public health)

  • In an epidemic, symptoms of several patients are pooled to form one picture, and the remedy matching it is the genus epidemicus (Organon §100–102).

  • Hahnemann used Belladonna as a prophylactic in scarlet fever (1799). This is the classic historical example.

Demography

Demography studies the size, composition and distribution of a population. Its three processes are fertility, mortality and migration.

The population grows fastest when the death rate has fallen but the birth rate is still high; India's birth rate is now falling, which places it in stage 3.

  • Census 2011 (latest completed census): population 121.09 crore; sex ratio 943 females per 1,000 males; child sex ratio (0–6 years) 919; literacy 74.04%; density 382 per sq km; decadal growth 17.7%.
  • Gujarat's sex ratio was 919.
  • The census is conducted under the Census Act, 1948.

Biostatistics essentials

  • Data types: qualitative (sex, blood group) versus quantitative; quantitative is discrete (number of children) or continuous (height, Hb).

  • Mean is affected by extreme values; median is the middle value and is best for skewed data; mode is the most frequent value.

  • Measures of dispersion: range, mean deviation, standard deviation (most used), variance (SD²), coefficient of variation = SD ÷ mean × 100.

  • Normal curve: bell-shaped, mean = median = mode. Mean ± 1 SD covers 68%, ± 2 SD covers 95%, ± 3 SD covers 99.7% of values.

  • Standard error of mean = SD ÷ √n.

  • Tests: chi-square for proportions (qualitative data); t-test for two means (small samples); z-test for large samples; ANOVA for three or more means.

  • p < 0.05 means the result is statistically significant. A type I error (α) is rejecting a true null hypothesis; a type II error (β) is accepting a false one.

  • Correlation coefficient (r) ranges from −1 to +1; it is shown on a scatter diagram.

  • Diagrams: bar chart and pie chart for qualitative data; histogram and frequency polygon for continuous data; ogive for cumulative frequency; line diagram for a trend over time.

Water

  • Safe water: free from pathogens and harmful chemicals, pleasant to drink, usable for domestic purposes. The daily need is about 150–200 litres per person in cities.

  • Purification on a large scale: storage → filtration → chlorination.

  • Slow sand filter: the vital layer (Schmutzdecke) of algae and bacteria on the sand surface does the work. Rapid sand filter: needs prior coagulation with alum; cleaned by back-washing.

  • Chlorination: the free residual chlorine must be 0.5 mg/L after 1 hour of contact. Break-point chlorination is the point after which added chlorine appears as free residual chlorine.

  • Horrock's apparatus finds the dose of bleaching powder for well water. Bleaching powder contains about 33% available chlorine.

  • Fluoride: desirable level 0.5–0.8 mg/L; above 1.5 mg/L causes fluorosis; correction is by the Nalgonda technique.

  • Hardness: temporary hardness (bicarbonates) is removed by boiling; permanent hardness (sulphates, chlorides) by the base-exchange process.

  • Noise above 85 dB risks hearing loss over long exposure.

Death and its signs

  • Somatic death: permanent stop of heart, lungs and brain function. Molecular (cellular) death follows over the next hours.

  • Brain-stem death is recognised for organ donation under the Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act, 1994.

  • Suspended animation: apparent death (e.g. drowning, electrocution, newborns); resuscitation must be attempted.

Post-mortem changes

Estimate the time since death by reading several changes together; no single sign is reliable alone.

  • Hypostasis (livor mortis): blood settles in dependent parts; spared at pressure points (contact flattening). It is cherry-red in CO and cyanide poisoning and chocolate (brown) in nitrite or potassium chlorate poisoning.

  • Rigor mortis: follows Nysten's rule: eyelids → face → neck → trunk → upper limbs → lower limbs, and passes off in the same order. The heart (myocardium) stiffens first of all.

  • Cadaveric spasm (instantaneous rigor): stiffening at the moment of death in a tense person, e.g. weeds clutched in drowning or a weapon gripped in suicide. It cannot be faked and is strong evidence of what happened at death.

  • Heat stiffening (burns, above 65 °C) and cold stiffening are different from rigor.

  • Putrefaction: the first external sign is greenish discolouration over the right iliac fossa (caecum lies close to skin). Casper's dictum: decay in air, water and earth is in the ratio 1 : 2 : 8.

  • Adipocere (saponification): body fat turns soapy in warm, moist conditions (water, damp graves). Mummification: drying in hot, dry air; features are preserved for identification.

Identification

  • Locard's exchange principle: every contact leaves a trace.

  • Fingerprints (dactylography): Galton's system; loops are the most common pattern; no two persons share fingerprints.

  • Age: from teeth (Gustafson's method) and ossification of bones; the third molar erupts at 17–25 years.

  • Sex: pelvis is the best bone, then skull.

  • DNA profiling is the most accurate method today.

Snake bite

  • India's "big four": common cobra, common krait, Russell's viper, saw-scaled viper.

  • Elapids (cobra, krait) are neurotoxic: ptosis, difficulty swallowing, respiratory paralysis. Krait bites at night and is often painless.

  • Vipers are haemotoxic: local swelling and bleeding. The 20-minute whole blood clotting test shows non-clotting blood.

  • First aid: reassure, immobilise the limb, no cutting or sucking, and transfer quickly for polyvalent anti-snake venom, which covers the big four.

Memory hook: the new laws keep their initials: Nyaya replaces the penal code, Nagarik Suraksha replaces the CrPC, Sakshya replaces the Evidence Act (BNS, BNSS, BSA from 1 July 2024). A hanging mark climbs obliquely above the thyroid and breaks; a strangulation mark runs flat and complete at or below it.

Cardinal principles of ethics

  • Autonomy (respect the patient's choice), beneficence (do good), non-maleficence (do no harm, primum non nocere), justice (fairness in care).

  • International codes: Hippocratic Oath; Declaration of Geneva (1948), the modern physician's pledge; Helsinki (1964), research on human subjects; Tokyo (1975), doctors must not take part in torture; Sydney (1968), determination of death; Oslo (1970), therapeutic abortion.

  • Organon §1: the physician's high and only mission is to restore the sick to health. §2: the ideal cure is rapid, gentle and permanent.

Duties and misconduct

  • Duties to the patient: attend in an emergency, keep confidentiality, obtain consent, keep records, give proper prescriptions with the registration number, and refer when beyond one's competence.

  • Professional ("infamous") misconduct includes: adultery or improper conduct with a patient; issuing false certificates; advertising or self-promotion; fee-splitting (dichotomy); covering an unqualified person; disclosing a patient's secrets without cause; performing an illegal abortion or sex determination; practising another system of medicine without qualification.

  • Penalties: warning or censure; temporary removal; penal erasure of the name from the register.

  • Cross-practice: in Poonam Verma v. Ashwin Patel (1996) the Supreme Court held that a homoeopath prescribing allopathic drugs without qualification is negligent per se.

Consent

  • Implied consent: the patient's conduct shows agreement (e.g. coming to the clinic, holding out an arm). It covers only a general examination.

  • Expressed consent: oral or written; written consent is needed for any procedure with risk.

  • Informed consent: the patient is told the nature, risks, alternatives and consequences of refusal, in a language they understand.

  • Age: a person aged 18 years or more can give valid consent (IPC 87). For a child under 12 or a person of unsound mind, the guardian consents (IPC 89). In an emergency, the doctor may act in good faith without consent (IPC 92). The BNS sections 25–30 carry these rules forward.

  • Blanket consent ("any treatment") is not valid.

  • A medico-legal examination of a victim needs that person's consent; an accused may be examined at police request without consent (BNSS 51, formerly CrPC 53).

Professional negligence

  • Elements, the "4 Ds": Duty of care, Dereliction (breach) of that duty, Direct causation, Damage.

  • Civil negligence brings compensation; criminal negligence needs gross negligence or recklessness (IPC 304A, now BNS 106).

  • Bolam test: a doctor is not negligent if a responsible body of fellow practitioners would have acted the same way.

  • Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005): guidelines that protect doctors from frivolous criminal prosecution.

  • Res ipsa loquitur ("the thing speaks for itself"): the facts prove negligence, e.g. a swab left in the abdomen or the wrong limb operated on.

  • Contributory negligence: the patient's own carelessness (e.g. not following advice) reduces the doctor's liability.

  • Novus actus interveniens: a new, unrelated act breaks the chain of causation.

  • Consumer protection: Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995) brought paid medical service under consumer law. Under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, claims go to the District Commission (up to ₹50 lakh), State Commission (₹50 lakh to ₹2 crore) or National Commission (above ₹2 crore).

Confidentiality and records

  • Professional secrecy: the doctor must not disclose what the patient tells them.

  • Privileged communication: disclosure is justified when the law or the public interest requires it, e.g. notifiable diseases, a court order, a patient's infection endangering a spouse, or a danger to others (e.g. an epileptic bus driver).

  • Records: keep in-patient records for at least 3 years; keep medico-legal records until the case is closed. A patient (or their legal heir) may ask for copies, which should be given within 72 hours.

  • The Charter of Patients' Rights (NHRC, 2019) lists rights such as information, records, emergency care, informed consent, confidentiality, a second opinion, non-discrimination, and choice of where to buy medicines and tests.

Legal procedure and evidence

  • Inquest: an inquiry into an unnatural death. Police inquest is the commonest in India; magistrate inquest is mandatory for deaths in custody, dowry deaths within 7 years of marriage and exhumation.

  • Medico-legal autopsy: ordered by the police or magistrate; relatives' consent is not needed. A clinical (academic) autopsy needs the relatives' consent.

  • Dying declaration: a statement by a dying person about the cause of death; best recorded by a magistrate, but a doctor may record it if death is imminent. The doctor certifies that the person is conscious and of sound mind (compos mentis). No oath is given. A dying deposition is recorded by a magistrate in the presence of the accused, with cross-examination, and carries more weight.

  • Witness examination order: examination-in-chief → cross-examination → re-examination. Leading questions are allowed only in cross-examination.

  • Perjury: giving false evidence under oath (IPC 191, now BNS 227).

  • Summons and conduct money: a doctor must obey a court summons; in a criminal case it cannot be refused for lack of fees.
