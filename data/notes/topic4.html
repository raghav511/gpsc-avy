Repertory is one of the ten Core topics (the Core section carries 25 of the 60 CBRT marks), so expect about 2 to 3 questions. The GPSC syllabus splits it into four headings, and this guide follows them in order:

  1. 4.1 History and structure: from Boenninghausen to Kent, then synthetic and computer repertories (RADAR, Synthesis).

  2. 4.2 Types: General, Purposive/Particular, Clinical and Card.

  3. 4.3 Methods: Kentian, Boenninghausen totality and concordance, elimination, rubric conversion.

  4. 4.4 Symptom evaluation: the hierarchy and rubric differentiation.

  • Each section ends with a coloured memory hook: say it aloud once, and it will carry the facts.
  • The pictures are drawn to be recalled as pictures.
  • The one-glance recall card near the end is for the last ten minutes before the exam.
  • Standard books: S. K. Tiwari, Essentials of Repertorisation; Kent's Repertory (6th American edition); Boenninghausen's Therapeutic Pocket Book (T. F. Allen edition); Boger's BBCR.
  • The fuller rapid-revision doc you already have, Homoeopathic Repertory – Rapid Revision, stays unchanged and goes deeper on rubric locations.

  • A repertory (Latin repertorium, a storehouse where things can be found) is an index of the symptoms of the Materia Medica.
  • It is a means to an end: the final choice is always confirmed in the Materia Medica.
  • A rubric (Latin rubrica, red) is a heading; old books printed headings in red ink.

Recall the picture as a snake: two Boenninghausen boxes, two Kent-line boxes, Boger turning the corner, then the three modern books.

The big three

  • Boenninghausen's TPB (1846). It has 7 sections: Mind and intellect; Parts of the body; Sensations and complaints; Sleep and dreams; Fever; Aggravations and ameliorations (modalities); Relationship of remedies (concordance).
  • It has 5 grades and about 125 remedies in the original (342 in Allen's edition).
  • Its ideas are the complete symptom (Location + Sensation + Modality + Concomitant), grand generalisation (a modality found in one part belongs to the whole patient) and the doctrine of analogy (fill a gap from similar symptoms elsewhere).

BBCR (C. M. Boger, 1905). Boger Boenninghausen's Characteristics and Repertory is a Materia Medica plus a repertory on Boenninghausen's plan, with more particular rubrics. Boger added causation, time and pathological generals (for example a tendency to suppuration or haemorrhage).

  • Kent's Repertory (1897–99). Built on Lippe's repertory, Kent's has 37 chapters from Mind to Generalities in Hahnemann's head-to-foot order.
  • It has 3 grades: bold = 3, italics = 2, roman = 1.
  • The plan runs from general to particular (Swedenborg's influence: the inner is above the outer).
  • Within a rubric, sub-rubrics run Side → Time → Conditions (modalities) → Extension → Location → Character (sensation).
  • Times run morning, forenoon, noon, afternoon, evening, night, midnight.
  • Desires, aversions and thirst sit in Stomach; food aggravations sit in Generalities.
  • Editions: 1st 1897; 2nd 1908; 3rd 1924, after Kent's death in 1916; 6th American 1957.

Modern and computer repertories

  • Synthetic Repertory (Barthel and Klunker) covers only general symptoms, compiled from many sources: Vol I Mind, Vol II Generals, Vol III Sleep, Dreams and Sexuality.

  • Synthesis (Schroyens) is Kent's 6th edition corrected and enlarged, with author references and 4 grades. It runs in RADAR (Rapid Aid to Drug Aimed Research; Archibel, Belgium).

  • Complete Repertory (van Zandvoort) is the database in MacRepertory (Kent Homeopathic Associates, USA).

  • Hompath was developed in India by Dr Jawahar Shah. ISIS is by David Witko.

  • Computers give speed, many repertories at once and many analysis strategies. But they are only as good as the rubrics chosen: garbage in, garbage out.

Memory hook: "Boenninghausen built the pocket, Boger filled it with time, Kent climbed from Mind to Generalities." Then the numbers in a chant: TPB 7 and 5, Kent 37 and 3, Knerr 48 and 4, Gentry 6 volumes.

Read each branch as a verb: if an MCQ names a book, ask what it does (plan, quote, zoom, name, punch or compute) and the type follows.

By arrangement

  • Schematic or anatomical (Hahnemann's head-to-foot order): Kent, Knerr, BBCR.

  • Alphabetical: Gentry (by key word), Phatak (by rubric), Murphy (by chapter), Clarke.

  • The classic two-way split is the logico-utilitarian group (symptoms reshaped to fit the book's plan: Kent, TPB, BBCR) against the Puritan group (Gentry, Knerr).

How a card repertory works

  • Each card carries one rubric, and each remedy has a fixed numbered position on every card.
  • A hole is punched where the remedy is present.
  • Stack the cards for the chosen rubrics and hold them to the light: holes that pass through every card are the remedies common to all rubrics.
  • It is quick and needs no counting.
  • Its limits: it ignores grades, and one small rubric can wrongly drop the true remedy.
  • Marcos Jimenez first brought grading into cards; Field's cards are based on Kent.

Clinical and regional repertories, in one line each

  • Clinical: quick in acute and one-sided cases, but prescribing on the disease name ignores individualisation, so it should never be the only basis. G. H. G. Jahr is called the father of the clinical repertory.

  • Regional: useful when one region dominates and generals are scanty; its remedy lists for that field are richer than a general repertory's.

Memory hook: "Generals Plan, Puritans Quote, Purposives Zoom, Clinics Name, Cards Punch." One verb per type: the general repertory plans (a philosophy), the Puritan quotes the prover, the purposive zooms into one part, the clinical names the disease, and the card punches holes.

Repertorisation means finding the remedy, or the small group of remedies, that covers the totality of the case with the help of a repertory. The final choice is made by studying the shortlist in the Materia Medica.

The steps, in order

  1. Case taking (recording)

  2. Analysis of the case (classify the symptoms)

  3. Evaluation of symptoms (grade their importance)

  4. Erecting the repertorial totality

  5. Choosing the repertory and method that suit the case

  6. Rubric conversion (patient's words into repertory language)

  7. Repertorisation (working out the rubrics)

  8. Repertorial result, then differentiation in the Materia Medica, then the prescription

In a case-based MCQ, find what the case is rich in, walk down the ladder and stop at the first yes.

Kent's working rules

  • Take the strongest mental and physical generals first, then the particulars that are peculiar.

  • If no exact rubric exists, go to the larger general rubric: Kent's book is built to be used general first.

  • There are two ways to count: numerical (how many rubrics a remedy covers) and qualitative (the sum of grades 3, 2 and 1). The totals guide the choice; they do not dictate it.

Elimination: the trap MCQs test

  • The eliminating rubric should be a characteristic general that is certain and of reasonable size.
  • Never eliminate with a small particular rubric or a doubtful symptom: the true remedy may be missing only because the rubric is incomplete.
  • Cascading elimination means each rubric eliminates for the next, all through the exercise.

Rubric conversion

Rubric conversion turns the patient's language into the repertory's language without changing the meaning.

  • "I feel worse when people try to comfort me" becomes Mind – Consolation – aggravates.

  • "I am scared to be alone at home" becomes Mind – Fear – alone, of being.

  • "My headache is better when I press hard" becomes Head – Pain – pressure – amel.

Use synonyms and cross-references, choose the rubric that matches the exact meaning, and when unsure take the broader rubric. Know where things sit: in Kent, thirst and food desires are in Stomach.

Memory hook: "Kent climbs down, Boenninghausen spreads out, Boger asks why and when." Kent goes from the top (mind) down to the parts; Boenninghausen spreads one modality over the whole patient; Boger starts with the cause and the time.

Evaluation decides which symptoms lead the repertorisation. The most-tested idea: characteristic (strange, rare, peculiar) symptoms rank highest and common diagnostic symptoms rank lowest (Organon §153).

Kent's hierarchy, high to low

  1. Mental generals, in their own order: Will (emotions: loves, hates, fears, desires) above Understanding (intellect: delusions, confusion) above Memory.

  2. Physical generals, the things the patient says of "I": thermal state, appetite, cravings and aversions, thirst, sleep and dreams, sexual sphere and menses, perspiration, time and weather modalities.

  3. Particulars, the things said of a part: peculiar particulars count; common particulars count least.

Picture a pyramid: the narrow top decides the remedy, the wide base only names the disease.

A strange, rare and peculiar symptom outranks this order wherever it sits.

Who ranks what first

  • Boenninghausen: the complete symptom; concomitants are most valuable; modalities can be generalised.

  • Kent: mentals first, then physical generals, then particulars.

  • Boger: causation and time first, then pathological generals, then modalities and mentals.

Rubric differentiation (Kent)

  • Consolation aggravates (Nat-m, Sep, Ign, Sil) against Consolation ameliorates (Puls, Phos).

  • Anxiety is vague uneasiness, Fear is of a definite thing, Anguish is extreme anxiety.

  • Irritability (easily annoyed), Anger (an outburst) and Violent are three different rubrics.

  • Forsaken feeling is a Mind rubric; Delusion, deserted is a false belief.

  • A delusion is a fixed false idea; fancies are imaginations the patient knows are not real.

  • Right side in a particular chapter refers to that part; Sides, right in Generalities refers to the whole body.

Memory hook: "Will before Wit before Memory; I before My." Emotions outrank intellect, which outranks memory; what the patient says of I (a general) outranks what is said of my knee (a particular). And SRP beats them all.
