1.1 Fundamental principles and pioneers

Homoeopathy rests on a few cardinal principles set out by Samuel Hahnemann in six editions of the Organon (1810 to 1842) and developed by Kent, Close and Roberts. Questions ask dates, edition details, aphorism numbers and who wrote which book.

1.2 Aphoristic teachings

The 291 aphorisms of the 6th edition follow one plan: the physician's mission, then disease, then medicines, then how medicines are applied. The map below places each block; the table after it holds the single aphorisms asked most.

The six concepts named in the syllabus

  • Health (§9): the vital force keeps all parts in harmonious operation so the mind can use the body for the higher purposes of existence.

  • Disease (§§11 to 12): a dynamic derangement of the vital force, shown outwardly only through signs and symptoms.

  • Cure (§§2, 17): removal of the whole totality of symptoms, rapidly, gently and permanently. Kent separates cure from recovery, the return to health once a cause is removed.

  • Indisposition (§150): a slight, passing complaint from an obvious cause such as a dietary error. Remove the cause; medicine is not needed.

  • Drug proving (§§105 to 145): one pure drug at a time (§122), on healthy people of both sexes (§127), in the 30th potency (§128), with a controlled diet (§125), on reliable provers (§126). Many provers are needed for a complete picture (§135). In India these are now called homoeopathic pathogenetic trials and are run by the CCRH.

  • Unprejudiced observer (§6): sees without theory or bias, noting only what the senses perceive; the first quality §83 asks of a case taker.

Memory hook: health is harmony (§9), disease is derangement (§11), cure is removal of the totality (§17), indisposition is removal of the cause (§150).

1.3 Case taking, evaluation, second prescription and posology

The exam asks how a case is taken, which symptoms rank highest, how to read the response to the first remedy (Kent's 12 observations) and how dose and repetition are decided.

Hahnemann's rules for case taking (§§83 to 104)

  1. Bring freedom from prejudice, sound senses, attention in observing and fidelity in tracing the picture (§83).

  2. Let the patient tell the story without interruption; then hear relatives and attendants (§84).

  3. Write in the patient's own words, each symptom on a separate line, leaving space to add details.

  4. Complete each symptom: where, what sensation, what makes it better or worse, what accompanies it.

  5. Never ask a question that suggests the answer or can be answered with a bare yes or no (§87).

  6. Add what the physician observes: appearance, behaviour, expression (§90).

  7. In chronic cases ask about small complaints the patient no longer mentions (§95).

  8. Acute cases are easier: the changes are recent and striking (§99).

  9. In an epidemic, combine several patients' symptoms to find the genus epidemicus (§§100 to 102).

Evaluation of symptoms (highest to lowest)

  1. Strange, rare and peculiar symptoms (§153).

  2. Mental generals, in Kent's order: will and emotions, then understanding, then memory.

  3. Physical generals: heat and cold, appetite, thirst, desires and aversions, sleep, sweat, sexual and menstrual function.

  4. Particulars: symptoms of one part, best when complete.

  5. Common symptoms of the disease diagnosis: lowest value.

A general symptom is said of 'I' (I am chilly); a particular of a part (my knee aches). Boenninghausen's complete symptom = location + sensation + modality + concomitant. Guernsey's keynote = one prominent feature pointing to a remedy. Causation ('ailments from' grief, a fall) ranks high when clear.

Memory hook: S-M-P-P-C: Strange first, then Mind, Physical generals, Particulars, Common last.

Memory hook: 1 to 4 = aggravation (bad to best), 5 to 7 = quality of relief, 8 to 9 = sensitive people, 10 to 12 = which symptoms appear. Old returning = good (11); wrong direction = antidote (12).

The second prescription (Kent)

  • Wait while improvement continues; repeating too soon spoils the cure.

  • Repeat the same remedy when improvement has stopped and the same symptoms return.

  • Antidote when symptoms take the wrong direction or a violent, needless aggravation occurs.

  • Complementary remedy when the first has done all it can and the picture has shifted to a related remedy.

  • Change the remedy only when the totality has clearly changed or the first prescription was wrong.

  • An intercurrent remedy (often a nosode) is given when reaction is deficient or a miasm blocks progress.

Posology

  • Minimum dose (§§275 to 279): just enough for a gentle cure; too large a dose causes needless aggravation. The dose can hardly be too small (§279).

  • Potency choice: greater susceptibility calls for higher potencies (Close). Mental and functional states and sensitive patients take higher potencies; advanced pathology, weak vitality and phlegmatic types take lower ones.

  • Kent's series: 30, 200, 1M, 10M, 50M, CM, then DM and MM.

  • Repetition: acute cases may need frequent doses. In the 5th edition a chronic case gets a single dose and the physician waits; the 6th edition allows repeated LM doses in water, succussed before each dose.

  • LM scale: 1 in 50,000 per step, 100 succussions; one drop medicates 500 poppy-seed globules. The 5th edition used 2 succussions per potency.

  • Homoeopathic aggravation is the primary action of a well-chosen remedy; a strong one means the dose was too large.

  • 12C (24X) is roughly where Avogadro's number is passed and no molecule of the original substance is likely to remain.

Memory hook: W-R-A-C-C: Wait, Repeat, Antidote, Complement, Change: the five choices of the second prescription.

1.4 Chronic diseases and miasms

Hahnemann traced chronic disease to three infectious miasms (psora, syphilis and sycosis), with psora behind about seven-eighths of all chronic disease. Questions test how the theory developed and the features that separate the miasms.

How the theory developed

  • Hahnemann saw chronic diseases treated homoeopathically keep returning in new forms. He studied the problem from about 1816, explained it to his pupils Stapf and Gross in 1827, and published The Chronic Diseases in 1828.

  • Acute miasms run a course and end. Some, such as smallpox, measles and whooping cough, occur once in a lifetime and are fixed miasms. Kent divides an acute miasm into prodromal, progressive and declining periods.

  • Chronic miasms never end on their own; the vital force cannot throw them off.

  • Three stages of infection: the moment of infection; the whole organism becomes diseased internally; then the outward local sign (itch eruption, chancre or fig-wart), which quiets the internal disease.

  • Suppression of the local sign drives the disease inwards to more vital organs; this is why Hahnemann opposed external applications.

  • Psora (from suppressed itch) is the oldest, most universal and most destructive miasm, the 'mother' of chronic disease (§80). It can stay latent for years.

  • Pseudo-psora is J. H. Allen's name for the tubercular diathesis, a mix of psora and syphilis.

  • One-word keys (P. S. Ortega): psora = deficiency, sycosis = excess, syphilis = destruction.

Picture the gauge: psora is the near-empty one, sycosis overflows, syphilis is broken, and the tubercular arrows never settle.
