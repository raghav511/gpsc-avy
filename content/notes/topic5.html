5.2a Cardiovascular physiology

Three things carry most marks: the order of events in the cardiac cycle, the normal ECG intervals, and the baroreceptor and RAAS control of blood pressure.

Cardiac cycle (heart rate 75/min, cycle 0.8 s)

Systole lasts 0.27–0.3 s and diastole about 0.5 s, so diastole shortens most when the heart rate rises.

PhaseValvesWhat happensDuration
1. Atrial systoleAV open, semilunar closedAtria top up the ventricles (about 20–30% of filling); a-wave in JVP; S4 if the ventricle is stiff0.1 s
2. Isovolumetric contractionAll four closedPressure rises, volume constant; begins with closure of AV valves (S1); c-wave in JVP0.05 s

Key numbers. End-diastolic volume 120–130 mL; end-systolic volume 50–60 mL; stroke volume about 70 mL; ejection fraction (SV/EDV) 55–70%; cardiac output = SV × HR = about 5 L/min; cardiac index about 3.2 L/min/m².

Heart sounds. S1 "lub" = closure of mitral and tricuspid valves, start of systole. S2 "dub" = closure of aortic and pulmonary valves, end of systole; it splits on inspiration (physiological splitting). S3 = rapid ventricular filling. S4 = atrial contraction into a stiff ventricle (always abnormal).

Laws. Frank–Starling: the greater the stretch (EDV), the stronger the contraction. Laplace: wall tension = pressure × radius / (2 × wall thickness), so a dilated heart works harder.

Conducting system and ECG

SA node (pacemaker, 70–80/min) → atria → AV node (delay of about 0.1 s, so atria empty first; intrinsic rate 40–60/min) → bundle of His → right and left bundle branches → Purkinje fibres (fastest conduction, 4 m/s; intrinsic rate 20–40/min).

Regulation of blood pressure

Mean arterial pressure = diastolic pressure + one third of pulse pressure (about 93 mmHg at 120/80). Blood pressure = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance; resistance is set mainly by the arterioles (Poiseuille: flow varies with the fourth power of the radius).

5.2b Respiratory physiology

The exam asks the muscles of breathing, the lung volumes, surfactant and compliance, and how O₂ and CO₂ are carried.

Mechanics of breathing

  • Inspiration is active. The diaphragm (phrenic nerve, C3–C5: "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive") does about 75% of quiet breathing; external intercostals lift the ribs. Forced inspiration adds sternocleidomastoid, scalenes and pectorals.

  • Quiet expiration is passive, from elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall. Forced expiration uses the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.

  • Pressures. Intrapleural pressure is about −2.5 to −3 mmHg at the end of expiration and about −6 mmHg at the end of inspiration; it keeps the lungs expanded. Air enters because alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric (about −1 mmHg).

  • Compliance = change in volume ÷ change in pressure (about 200 mL/cmH₂O for lungs alone). Falls in fibrosis and pulmonary oedema; rises in emphysema.

  • Surfactant (dipalmitoyl phosphatidylcholine), secreted by type II alveolar cells from about 24 weeks of gestation, lowers surface tension, keeps small alveoli open (Laplace: P = 2T/r) and prevents oedema. Lack of it in preterm babies = respiratory distress syndrome (hyaline membrane disease).

  • Airway resistance is highest in the medium-sized bronchi. Sympathetic (β₂) stimulation dilates bronchi; parasympathetic (vagus) constricts them.

Oxygen–haemoglobin dissociation curve (sigmoid). Shift to the right (Hb gives up O₂ more easily): raised CO₂, H⁺ (low pH: the Bohr effect), temperature and 2,3-BPG, as in exercising muscle. Control of breathing

The dorsal respiratory group in the medulla sets the inspiratory rhythm; the pneumotaxic centre in the pons limits inspiration. Central chemoreceptors (medulla) respond to H⁺ in the CSF, that is, to arterial CO₂: the main moment-to-moment drive. Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid bodies via IX, aortic bodies via X) respond mainly to low arterial PO₂ (below about 60 mmHg). The Hering–Breuer reflex (stretch receptors, vagus) stops over-inflation.

Follow the arrows: filtration at the glomerulus, bulk reabsorption in the PCT (highlighted), concentration and dilution in the loop, and hormone-controlled fine-tuning in the distal tubule and collecting duct.

Autoregulation. GFR and renal blood flow stay nearly constant between mean arterial pressures of about 80 and 180 mmHg, through the myogenic response and tubuloglomerular feedback.

Hormones the kidney makes. Renin, erythropoietin (from peritubular interstitial cells; falls in chronic kidney disease, causing anaemia), and calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxy vitamin D, made by 1α-hydroxylase in the proximal tubule).

Micturition. A spinal reflex at S2–S4, controlled by the pons. Parasympathetic (pelvic splanchnic nerves) contracts the detrusor; sympathetic relaxes it; the external sphincter is voluntary (pudendal nerve). The urge is first felt at about 150 mL and becomes marked at 400 mL.

5.3b Endocrine: hypothalamic-pituitary axis and feedback loops

The hypothalamus sends releasing hormones down the portal veins to the anterior pituitary, the anterior pituitary drives the target glands, and the target-gland hormone switches both off. That loop, negative feedback, answers most questions.

Posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis). Stores hormones made in the hypothalamus (supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei) and sent down axons. ADH (vasopressin): water reabsorption in the collecting duct; lack = diabetes insipidus (large volumes of dilute urine). Oxytocin: milk ejection (let-down) and uterine contraction in labour.

Feedback types. Negative feedback (most hormones): the product switches off its own drive. Positive feedback (rare): the LH surge before ovulation (rising oestrogen), oxytocin in labour (Ferguson reflex), clotting cascade.

In hypothyroidism from a failing thyroid, T4 falls, feedback is lost, and TSH rises; that is why a high TSH is the first sign of primary hypothyroidism.

Adrenal cortex hook: "Salt, Sugar, Sex — the deeper you go, the sweeter it gets" (glomerulosa, fasciculata, reticularis: "GFR"). The cortex comes from mesoderm, the medulla from neural crest.

Thyroid facts. Iodide is trapped by the sodium–iodide symporter, oxidised by thyroid peroxidase and attached to thyroglobulin. T4 is the main product; T3 is 3–5 times more potent and is mostly made from T4 in the tissues. Normal TSH is about 0.4–4.5 mIU/L.

Calcium. Normal serum calcium 9–11 mg/dL (about half ionised). PTH and vitamin D raise it; calcitonin lowers it.

The Na⁺–K⁺ ATPase (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP) keeps this difference. Normal plasma osmolality is 285–295 mOsm/kg; estimated as 2 × Na⁺ + glucose/18 + urea/2.8 (mg/dL). 0.9% NaCl is isotonic with plasma.

Starling forces decide movement between plasma and interstitium: capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out; plasma oncotic pressure (albumin, about 25–28 mmHg) pulls it in. Oedema follows high venous pressure (heart failure), low albumin (nephrotic syndrome, liver disease), leaky capillaries (inflammation) or blocked lymphatics (filariasis).

Water balance. Daily intake about 2.5 L (drink 1.5 L, food 0.8 L, metabolic water 0.3 L) equals output (urine 1.5 L, skin and lungs about 0.9 L as insensible loss, faeces 0.1 L). Thirst and ADH respond to a 1–2% rise in osmolality.

Hook: "ROME" — Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal. In respiratory disorders pH and PaCO₂ move in opposite directions; in metabolic disorders pH and HCO₃⁻ move in the same direction. 5.4b Clinical biochemistry: enzymes, lipid profile, blood glucose

For each test, know the normal range and the organ a raised value points to.

Enzymes

Basics. Enzymes are protein catalysts that lower activation energy; they do not change the equilibrium. Km (Michaelis constant) = substrate concentration at half the maximum velocity; a low Km means high affinity. Competitive inhibitors raise Km, Vmax unchanged; non-competitive inhibitors lower Vmax, Km unchanged. Isoenzymes catalyse the same reaction but differ in structure (for example, LDH and CK).

Six classes (IUBMB): "Over The HILL" — Oxidoreductases, Transferases, Hydrolases, Isomerases, Lyases, Ligases.
